# Highest-Income Opportunities at 40

A decision document, not a listicle. US-centric, figures current as of August 2026.
Not financial advice — verify numbers against your own market before committing capital.

---

## The only question that matters

"What makes the most money" has no single answer, because the top-earning path is
different depending on one input: **what you already have.** Ranking paths by their
income ceiling alone is how people at 40 end up four years into a credential they'll
never finish.

The right ranking is by **expected value**:

```
EV  =  income ceiling  ×  P(you actually get there)  ÷  years to first real dollar
       ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
                        capital and career risk you're putting up
```

At 40 you have roughly 25 working years left. That is long enough that a 2–4 year
rebuild pays back many times over — and short enough that an 8-year path with a
selective gate in the middle is a genuinely bad bet. Both halves of that sentence are
true, and most advice only tells you one of them.

Three things change at 40 relative to 25, and they all point the same direction:

1. **You have leverage you didn't have before** — credit history, a professional
   network, domain knowledge, and often home equity. The highest-EV paths below are
   the ones that *use* that leverage. Paths that ignore it put you in a starting
   cohort against 25-year-olds who will work for less.
2. **Time-to-income is now expensive.** Every year with no income is ~4% of your
   remaining career plus the compounding you didn't get.
3. **Some doors are literally age-locked.** See "Traps," below.

## The five routes, compared

| Route | Realistic earnings | Time to first real money | Capital needed | Risk of ruin | Best if you have |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Buy a cash-flowing business** | $150k–$500k+/yr owner earnings; plus a 3–8x equity event | 3–12 months to close, income day one | 10% of price down (5% must be real cash) | **High** — personally guaranteed debt | Credit, savings, management or ops experience |
| **2. Commission sales** | $150k–$300k OTE; top reps $350k–$500k+ | 6–18 months (ramp + first full comp cycle) | ~$0 | Low | Resilience, existing industry relationships |
| **3. Credentialed scarcity** (healthcare / licensed trades) | $95k–$225k, very durable | 2–4 years (some paths 6–8) | Tuition, or *paid* apprenticeship | Very low | Patience, ability to live on less for 2–4 years |
| **4. Monetize the expertise you already have** (fractional / consulting / advisory) | $150k–$400k; $1,500–$4,000/day at C-suite tier | 3–9 months | ~$0 | Low–medium (income lumpy) | 15+ years of a specialty someone pays to skip |
| **5. Escalate inside your current field** | +20–60% over 2–3 years | Immediate | $0 | Lowest | An existing career with headroom |

## Route 1 — Buy a business (highest ceiling, and the one most people don't know is open)

This is the answer for the largest number of 40-year-olds with decent credit, and it's
the least-known one. You are not starting a business. You are buying one that already
has customers, staff, and profit — typically from a retiring owner.

**The mechanics.** An SBA 7(a) loan is the standard instrument for acquisitions under
$5M. A bank lends the purchase price, the SBA guarantees most of it, and you inject
**10% equity — of which only half must be genuine non-borrowed cash** (the rest can be
seller-financed on standby). Terms in 2026: up to $5M, 10-year amortization, all-in
rates roughly 9–11% (Prime + 2–2.75%). Closing runs 45–75 days once you're under LOI.
SBA raised the cumulative 7(a)+504 ceiling to $10M in 2026, which matters if you intend
to buy more than one.

**Why the math works.** A business bought at 3x earnings throws off enough to service
the debt and pay you a salary from month one. You are buying an income stream with
mostly someone else's money — the single largest legal leverage available to a person
with no institutional backing.

**Where the money actually is.** Home services — HVAC, plumbing, electrical — is the
clearest example, because private equity has spent 8 years proving the model: over $50B
deployed into residential trades roll-ups since 2018, buying independents at 5–8x EBITDA
and re-selling platforms at 17–20x. Apollo put ~$2B into Apex Service Partners at a
~$10B valuation in May 2026. Regional platforms above $25M revenue now clear 9–13x.
You cannot buy at platform scale, but you *can* buy the $1–3M revenue independent that
platforms eventually acquire — and sell into that bid in 5–7 years. That second event,
not the salary, is where the life-changing number is.

**The honest downside.** This is the only route here with real risk of ruin. You will
personally guarantee the debt, and SBA loans are recourse. A business that loses its two
biggest customers post-close, or that turns out to have been running on the departing
owner's relationships, can take your house. Mitigations that actually work: quality of
earnings review before close (not just the seller's P&L), meaningful seller financing on
standby so the seller's incentive survives closing, customer concentration under ~15%,
and a business whose value sits in contracts, licenses, routes, or recurring service —
not in the owner's personality.

**Who should not do this:** anyone who has never managed people, and anyone whose down
payment is money they cannot afford to lose entirely.

## Route 2 — Commission sales (highest income per year of training, by a wide margin)

The only field where a 40-year-old with no new credential can be at $200k+ within 18
months.

- **Enterprise SaaS account executive** — $200k–$300k+ OTE typical, top performers
  $500k+. The highest-earning individual-contributor role in sales, period.
- **Medical device sales** — average total comp around $155k, experienced reps to
  ~$208k, top OTE around $348k. Degree helpful but frequently not required.

**Why 40 is an advantage here and nowhere else on this list.** Comp scales with deal
complexity — enterprise and clinical buyers are risk-averse, senior, and skeptical, and
they buy more readily from someone their own age with operating scars. Twenty years of
having been the *customer* in some industry is a genuine, sellable edge. The strongest
version of this move is selling into the industry you already came from.

**The honest downside.** Income is volatile, quota resets to zero every year, and a bad
territory or a mid-year comp-plan change can cost you a year. The first 6–12 months are
usually below your old salary. Roughly half of new AEs wash out. If your household
cannot absorb a 12-month dip, treat that as disqualifying rather than something to power
through.

## Route 3 — Credentialed scarcity (the safe high floor)

Slower, but these are the most *reliable* six figures on the list, and they're
recession- and AI-resistant in a way desk work is not.

**Healthcare, ranked by pay-to-training ratio:**

| Role | Median pay | Training from zero |
|---|---|---|
| Radiation therapist | ~$105k | 2-yr associate + certification |
| Diagnostic medical sonographer | ~$97k | 2-yr associate + credentialing (13% growth to 2034) |
| Nurse practitioner | ~$126k | BSN + MSN, ~4–6 yrs from zero |
| CRNA (nurse anesthetist) | ~$223k mean | **7–9 yrs from zero** — see below |

Radiation therapy and sonography are the best trade in the table: near-six-figures on a
two-year associate degree, which is the strongest pay-to-education ratio in American
healthcare. At 40, you'd be credentialed by 42 and earning at that level for 20+ years.

**On CRNA specifically:** the $223k is real, and the path is not what it looks like from
the outside. You need a BSN, then *at least* two years of ICU/ER experience as a
prerequisite to apply, then a doctorate (the master's route closed in 2025). From a
non-nursing start that is realistically 7–9 years, through a competitive admissions gate
in the middle, finishing at 47–49. It can be worth it — but it is a different category
of commitment from everything else here, and it's the single most common way a
"highest-paying job" list misleads a 40-year-old.

**Licensed trades** (earn while you train — apprenticeships are paid, so time-to-income
is roughly zero):

- Elevator installer/repairer — median ~$99k, top 10% ~$130k, 4–5 year IUEC apprenticeship
- Power lineman — median ~$82k, top 10% ~$117k, 3–4 year apprenticeship, large overtime
  during storm restoration

**The real play here isn't the wage — it's Route 1 in five years.** Journeyman wages cap
out around $100–130k. Owning the shop is what clears $300k+, and the trade license is
often the thing that makes you a credible buyer of a trades business. Trade first,
acquire second, is a coherent 8-year plan that ends where Route 1 ends.

## Route 4 — Sell the 20 years you already have

If you have deep expertise in anything a company pays to not have to learn, you can
usually re-price it upward by leaving the org chart:

- Independent specialist consulting: $150–$350/hr is the honest boutique-tier band;
  strategy and C-level advisory reaches $350–$500/hr
- Fractional executive retainers: $5k–$15k/month per client (junior to senior); a
  fractional Chief AI Officer engagement runs $5k–$30k/month
- Day rates for C-suite-caliber advisory: $1,500–$4,000/day

Three clients at $8k/month is ~$290k gross. That's the whole model, and the arithmetic
is the easy part.

**The honest downside.** This is a *sales* business wearing a consultant's clothes.
Practitioners who can't sell earn less than they did employed, and pipeline gaps are
brutal. The reliable version starts before you quit: build the first two clients while
still employed, and don't leave until one of them renews.

## What to avoid at 40 (specific, not generic)

- **Air traffic controller.** It tops nearly every "highest-paying job without a degree"
  list at ~$145k median. The FAA maximum hiring age is **31**, measured at your entry-on-
  duty date. The only exceptions are former FAA controllers and DoD/military controllers
  (to 36). Waiver requests from civilian applicants are essentially all denied. The lists
  do not mention this. It is the clearest example of why you check the gate before the
  salary.
- **Any path with a selective admissions gate more than 3 years out.** Not because it
  can't work, but because a rejection at 44 with the prerequisite years already spent has
  no cheap recovery.
- **Franchises pitched on "semi-absentee" ownership.** You pay a premium multiple *and*
  a royalty for a brand, and the semi-absentee promise is usually the part that fails.
  The same capital buys an existing independent with real cash flow and no royalty.
- **Trading, crypto, and "passive income" courses.** Every honest version of these has a
  worse expected value than Route 1 and a worse Sharpe ratio than an index fund. When
  someone's income comes from teaching the method rather than running it, that's the tell.
- **Starting a startup from zero.** Not because you're too old — because the risk-adjusted
  return is strictly worse than *buying* revenue when you have 25 years left instead of 45.
  Zero-to-one is a young person's arbitrage on time. Yours is on credit and judgment.

## Decision tree

**You have $50k–$150k liquid and decent credit** → Route 1. Highest EV available to you.
Start with searching, not financing; the deal is the hard part, the money isn't.

**You have no capital but strong interpersonal skill and 12 months of runway** → Route 2,
selling into the industry you already know. Fastest path to $200k with zero capital at risk.

**You have no capital and no runway** → Route 3, licensed trade (paid apprenticeship, income
from day one), then Route 1 in year 5–8. This is the highest-EV path for someone starting
from a genuinely standing start.

**You have 15+ years in a specialty** → Route 4 first, and use the consulting income to
fund Route 1. This pairing is the most under-used combination on the list.

**You already earn well and like your field** → Route 5, and put the surplus into Route 1
as a passive or semi-active investor. Don't blow up a good position for a listicle.

## First 90 days (whichever route)

1. **Weeks 1–2 — establish the real constraint.** Write down: liquid capital, monthly
   burn, months of runway, credit score, and the number of months your household can
   absorb reduced income. Every recommendation above is downstream of these five numbers.
   Most people skip this and then choose a path their finances were never going to survive.
2. **Weeks 3–6 — talk to five people already doing it.** Not content creators — operators.
   Ask what they earn, what it cost them, and what they'd do differently. This is the single
   highest-return activity in the whole document, and the step almost everyone omits.
3. **Weeks 7–10 — run a reversible test.** Route 1: get pre-qualified with an SBA lender
   and review 20 listings. Route 2: get three informational interviews with sales managers
   in your old industry. Route 3: talk to a program admissions officer and an apprenticeship
   coordinator about placement rates, not curriculum. Route 4: land one paid engagement
   while still employed.
4. **Weeks 11–13 — commit or discard, explicitly.** Set the decision date in advance and
   write down what would have to be true. Undated optionality is the most expensive thing
   on this list — it costs a year and feels like progress.
