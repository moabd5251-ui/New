# Subcontracting — Two Strategies, Two Different Problems

This is the best idea in the thread so far. It splits into two distinct moves that solve
different constraints, and the strongest plan uses both, in sequence.

---

## Path A — You subcontract *under* an established provider

You own an LLC, one vehicle, and commercial insurance. An established King County NEMT
company with more work than capacity feeds you trips. You drive them.

### What this fixes

**It resolves the question that could have sunk the whole plan.** The open risk in
`05-YEAR-ONE-PLAN.md` was whether Hopelink counts *private-pay* operation as "NEMT
experience." If your prime holds the Hopelink contract, **the trips you run are brokered
Medicaid NEMT trips** — experience in exactly the form Hopelink means. You stop guessing.

**It removes the year-one sales cycle.** No cold-calling dialysis centers for your first
revenue. The prime already has the contracts; you supply capacity. Revenue starts when your
insurance binds, not three months later when a facility finally signs.

**It keeps the capital gate at one vehicle.** Roughly **$1,300–$3,800**, not the
$10,400–$25,400 two-vehicle version. You're buying into an existing book instead of building
one.

**It still builds everything you need for year three:** trip logs, insurance loss runs,
driver credential files, safety record, corporate books. The documentation discipline in
`05-YEAR-ONE-PLAN.md` applies unchanged and matters just as much.

### The economics, and the number that decides it

Here's what the public data actually supports, and what it doesn't:

- **Direct facility contracts pay 15–25% more per trip** than broker-subcontracted rates.
  That's the price of not having to sell.
- **Company-to-company subcontract splits are negotiated, not published.** I could not find
  a reliable standard rate. Anyone quoting you a "typical" number is guessing.
- The **35–50% of trip revenue** figure you'll find online is *driver* pay under a
  percentage-of-collected model — a company billing $55 and paying a driver $22. **That is
  not your deal.** If a prime offers you 40% "like we pay drivers," they are proposing you
  work as a driver while personally carrying insurance, a vehicle, and B&O tax. Walk.

**The rule that tells you whether a subcontract offer is worth taking.** Your per-trip rate
has to beat what you'd earn as that same company's W-2 driver — because as an employee, the
insurance, vehicle, and taxes are *their* problem.

```
Minimum viable subcontract rate per trip
  = (your time × a fair driver wage)      e.g. 1.25 hrs × $25 = $31.25
  + fuel and maintenance                  ≈ $4.50
  + your fixed cost per trip              ≈ $897/mo ÷ 70 trips = $12.80
  ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  ≈ $48.50 per trip
```

**Below roughly $48–50 per trip, you are better off as their employee.** You'd earn similar
money with none of the risk, no insurance premium, no vehicle, no B&O. Run your own numbers
— but do the arithmetic *before* the conversation, not after you've signed.

Model it in the calculator by putting your offered subcontract rate into the private/facility
base rate and setting per-mile to zero if the deal is flat per trip.

### The three clauses to negotiate

1. **Non-compete and non-solicitation.** The prime will likely try to bar you from their
   facilities and possibly from contracting with Hopelink directly. **This is the single most
   important term in the agreement** — it decides whether year three exists. Push for a
   narrow non-solicit covering only their named existing accounts, time-limited, and
   explicitly *not* barring you from applying to Hopelink or brokers on your own.
2. **Whether their contract even permits sub-subcontracting.** Broker agreements sometimes
   prohibit it outright or require the broker's written approval of each subcontractor. If
   the prime is quietly violating their Hopelink contract by using you, you inherit the
   blast radius when it surfaces. **Ask to see the clause that authorizes it.**
3. **Termination and volume.** No minimum volume commitment means you carry an $8,000/year
   insurance premium against trips they may stop sending. Get a floor, a notice period, or
   both.

### The real risk

**Single-customer concentration.** One prime supplying all your volume is the same
structural risk as one broker — worse, because you're a layer further from the money and
easier to replace. **Run subcontract trips to pay the bills while you build direct facility
contracts on the side.** Direct work pays 15–25% more and it's yours. By month twelve you
want the prime to be a minority of your revenue, not all of it.

## Path B — You become the prime and subcontract owner-operators

This is the answer to the *other* problem: reaching five vehicles without buying four cars.
Instead of owning a fleet, you contract owner-operators who bring their own vehicles and
insurance, and run trips under your contracts.

### It only works if two things are true

**First: Hopelink has to count capacity rather than title.** Unresolved, and unanswerable
from public sources. It's question one on the list in `05-YEAR-ONE-PLAN.md`, and this whole
path lives or dies on it. **Ask before you build anything around it.**

**Second: the owner-operators have to genuinely pass Washington's ABC test.** This is where
Path B gets dangerous. Washington **presumes employment** and requires you to prove all three
prongs — including freedom from your direction and control *in practice*, not just on paper.
Misclassification exposure includes back L&I premiums, unemployment insurance, benefits, and
penalties.

An owner-operator has a materially better shot at passing than a driver in your van — they
own the vehicle, carry their own commercial policy, hold their own UBI, and can serve other
customers. But "better shot" is not "safe." **Do not build this structure without a
Washington employment attorney reviewing it.** A few hundred dollars of review against a
misclassification finding that reaches back years is the easiest cost-benefit call in this
entire document.

### What it buys you

If both conditions hold, Path B compresses the five-vehicle timeline from *year three or
four* down to *whenever you can recruit four owner-operators* — no vehicle purchases, no
payroll, no L&I on their hours. That is the difference between a capital problem and a
recruiting problem, and recruiting is the one you can solve with no money.

## The combined plan

This is what I'd actually do in your position:

| Phase | Move | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Months 1–3** | Subcontract under an established King County prime (Path A) | Revenue immediately, one-vehicle capital gate, and — if they hold the Hopelink contract — *qualifying brokered experience* rather than experience you have to argue for |
| **Months 3–12** | Build direct facility contracts alongside the subcontract work | 15–25% better rates, and it ends the single-customer dependency before it can hurt you |
| **Month 6** | Get Hopelink's written answer on the capacity-vs-title question | Determines whether Path B is available at all |
| **Year 2** | If capacity counts: recruit owner-operators toward five (Path B), attorney-reviewed | Turns a $50k capital problem into a recruiting problem |
| **Year 2–3** | Apply to Hopelink with a documented year, clean loss runs, and five vehicles' capacity | Arrive as a profitable operator, not a hopeful startup |

**This is strictly better than the two-vehicle plan.** Lower capital gate, faster revenue,
qualifying experience instead of maybe-qualifying experience, and it keeps the private-pay
book — the actually profitable part — at the center rather than treating it as a placeholder
until Hopelink arrives.

## What to ask a prime on the first call

You're interviewing them as much as they're interviewing you.

1. What do you pay a subcontracted provider per trip, and is it flat or base-plus-mileage?
2. Do you hold the Hopelink contract, or are you also a subcontractor? *(If they're also a
   sub, you're a third layer down and the economics almost certainly don't work.)*
3. Does your broker agreement permit subcontracting, and does Hopelink have to approve me?
4. What volume can you commit to, and what's the termination notice?
5. What insurance limits do you require me to carry?
6. What does the non-compete say about my contracting with facilities or brokers directly?
7. Will you provide a letter documenting my trips and service dates when I need it? *(Say
   this early. It's reasonable, it costs them nothing, and their reaction tells you whether
   they see you as a partner or a captive.)*

Question 7 is the quiet one. **The documentation is why you're doing this**, and a prime who
won't confirm your service history in writing is a prime who intends for you never to leave.
