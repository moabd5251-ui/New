# Bus Operator, 40, No Capital — The Real Options

Third and most specific document in this set. Supersedes the freight-brokerage
recommendation in `TRANSPORTATION_NO_CAPITAL_PLAYBOOK.md`, which assumed freight-side
domain knowledge (shipper contacts, lane pricing) that a bus operator does not have.
US-centric, August 2026.

---

## Correction to the previous recommendation

The brokerage argument rested on you already owning freight relationships. Driving a bus
doesn't produce those, so entering brokerage would mean starting cold against people who
have them — a worse bet than what's actually available to you.

**What you do own is more useful than it sounds:** a commercial driver's license with a
passenger endorsement, a DOT medical card, a documented safe-driving record, and
experience moving people rather than boxes. That combination is a licensed, scarce,
regulator-gated asset. The whole plan below is about getting paid what it's actually
worth, because most bus operators are not.

---

## First: which bus? The two situations are worth $50,000/year apart

This is the fork that determines everything:

| | Pay | Pension |
|---|---|---|
| **Union transit operator** (city/regional agency) | $70k–$117k with overtime | Defined-benefit pension |
| **School bus / private shuttle / non-union** | ~$44k avg; top 10% ~$59.5k | Usually none |

The gap is not skill. It is union representation, and the same license drives both.
A senior MTA operator earns around $90k while a driver at a non-union private shuttle
running comparable routes earns closer to $38k.

---

## Fork A — You're a union transit operator: **do not leave**

If you're at a major agency, you are already in a top-decile job for someone without a
degree, and most of this document's alternatives are lateral or worse.

Real 2026 numbers:
- **CTA (Chicago):** ~$117,574/yr working 50 hours/week, overtime at $61.67/hr
- **MTA (NYC):** starts $26.19/hr; operators regularly total **$95k–$115k** with overtime;
  15+ year veterans exceed $35/hr base
- **LA Metro:** $28.81/hr start, topping at $42.57/hr full-time after five years
- **NJ Transit:** top rate $33.99/hr; ~$97,405 with 10 hours of overtime weekly

**The pension is the part people undervalue.** A defined-benefit pension is not a perk,
it's an asset — the present value of a transit pension commonly runs into the high six
figures, and it is the single largest asset most operators will ever hold. Leaving at 40
forfeits the steepest accrual years. Any move that costs you the pension has to clear a
much higher bar than the salary comparison suggests.

**So the play is stacking, not leaving:**

1. **Seniority and overtime are your fastest raise.** The difference between $70k and
   $117k at the same agency is pick seniority and hours, not a different career. Learn how
   your agency's pick process actually works — that knowledge is worth more per hour spent
   than anything else on this page.
2. **Move to rail.** At LA Metro, bus and train operators share the same pay scale, but
   rail generally means better conditions and a cleaner path to supervision. King County
   Metro and MBTA both run formal bus-operator-to-rail-operator pathways.
3. **Then supervisor or instructor.** MBTA advances operators to instructor via inspector
   experience, and to training supervisor with five years of field operations plus
   supervisory time. This is the internal ladder that ends in management pay with the
   pension intact.
4. **Use the tuition benefit.** LA Metro and others offer tuition assistance; Houston METRO
   pays for CDL and free renewals. If you're going to add a credential, make your employer
   buy it.

Then build wealth *alongside* the job — see "The wealth path," below.

## Fork B — You're on a school bus or private shuttle: **the raise is one application away**

At ~$44k you are underpaid for the license you already hold, and the fix does not require
a training program. Two moves, in order of certainty:

**1. Apply to a union transit agency. This is the highest-certainty raise in any of these
three documents.** Same CDL class, same passenger endorsement, often the same vehicle
size — for 1.5x to 2.5x the money plus a pension. It is an application and a hiring
process, not a career change, and agencies are hiring operators continuously. School bus
experience with a clean record is exactly the profile they screen for. If you do nothing
else from this document, do this.

**2. Or upgrade CDL-B → CDL-A and go to freight.** Three to six weeks and roughly
$3,960–$5,582, and you must complete FMCSA-registered ELDT for the upgrade. Many carriers
reimburse or pay outright. Then add hazmat and tanker endorsements — tanker/hazmat is
generally the highest-paying company-driver work, and the specialized tiers (heavy haul,
oversize) clear $120k+. You already hold the license, the medical card, and the record, so
you're skipping the expensive part of that path.

Between the two: transit if you value the pension and a predictable schedule; freight if
you want the higher ceiling and don't mind time away. Transit is the better risk-adjusted
answer for most people at 40 with no capital.

## The wealth path (both forks): non-emergency medical transportation

Wages don't compound; a business does. NEMT is the version of "buy a business" that's
actually reachable from where you're standing, because it runs on exactly what you have —
a passenger-endorsed CDL, a safety record, and knowing how to move vulnerable people on a
schedule.

**The economics:**
- A single van produces **$120k–$250k/year in revenue**
- Net margins: **8–15%** on Medicaid/Medicare-Advantage broker trips, **20–35%** on private
  pay and direct healthcare-facility contracts, **12–22%** blended
- Market is ~$12.5B and demographically guaranteed to grow

**Starting with no capital.** Full startup runs $57k–$163k, largely because a wheelchair
van costs $43k–$71k. You don't start there. Start ambulatory-only with a used minivan,
keep the day job, run evenings and weekends, and chase **private-pay and facility contracts
rather than Medicaid brokers** — dialysis centers especially, because their patients travel
three times a week, forever, which is the closest thing to recurring revenue in this
business. That's the margin difference between 8% and 30%. Add a wheelchair-accessible
vehicle once contracts justify financing one, then a second driver, then you're out of the
van.

**The honest downsides.** Medicaid broker reimbursement is low and pays slowly — 30 to 90
days, which is a cash-flow problem when you have no cushion. Commercial passenger
insurance is expensive and it is the line item that kills undercapitalized operators.
Broker contracts can be re-bid away from you. And the "start an NEMT business" content
online is heavily grifted — the people selling the course make their money from the course.
Validate by calling three local facilities and asking what they currently pay per trip and
whether their provider is reliable, before you spend a dollar.

**Why this ends well:** service businesses with contracts and recurring routes are exactly
what gets acquired. Build it to two or three vans over five to seven years, keep clean
books, and you own something that sells — which is the equity the first document said
usually requires capital you don't have. It doesn't, if you build it from a license you
already hold.

## What not to do

- **Don't leave a union transit job for freight driving.** You'd trade a pension for a pay
  bump that overtime could have gotten you anyway.
- **Don't buy a truck or a wheelchair van as step one.** With no cushion, financing a
  $50k+ asset before you have contracts is how this fails. Contracts first, vehicles second.
- **Don't pay for a "how to start NEMT" program.** Free from your state Medicaid office and
  the brokers' own provider-enrollment pages.
- **Don't go straight into freight brokerage.** Correct for a freight dispatcher; not for
  you, yet. If you upgrade to Class A and run freight for a few years, it opens back up —
  and then the earlier document applies as written.

---

## First 90 days

1. **Week 1 — establish the fork.** Union transit or not. If not, your entire near-term
   plan is the transit application.
2. **Weeks 1–3 — apply.** *Fork B:* every transit agency within commuting distance, plus
   paratransit and municipal operators. *Fork A:* find out exactly how your agency's pick,
   overtime, and rail-transfer processes work, and what your pension accrues per additional
   year of service.
3. **Weeks 4–8 — validate NEMT locally, for free.** Call five dialysis centers, two assisted
   living facilities, and your state's Medicaid transportation broker. Ask: what do you pay
   per trip, who do you use now, are they reliable. Three answers about unreliable providers
   is a business.
4. **Weeks 8–12 — bank runway, then commit.** Every version of this needs three months of
   expenses behind it. Overtime is the fastest way there and it's available to you now.
5. **Decision date at month four.** Transit offer in hand → take it. NEMT validated with
   two interested facilities → register the LLC and price insurance. Neither → the CDL-A
   upgrade is the fallback, not another quarter of research.
