# How These Options Hold Up in a Downturn

Cross-cutting analysis of every path in this repo. August 2026.

---

## Where the economy actually is right now

Not in a recession, and not obviously heading into one — but slowing.

| Indicator | Reading |
|---|---|
| U.S. Bank recession probability, next 12 months | **25%** |
| NY Fed yield-curve model, 12 months ahead | **15.2%** |
| Market-implied odds *against* recession by year-end 2026 | **92.5%** |
| GDP growth, Q2 2026 | **1.5%**, down from 2.1% in Q1 |
| Unemployment | **4.3–4.6%**, layoffs historically low |
| Hiring | **Slowing** |

**The labor market detail matters more to you than the recession odds.** "Low layoffs, slow
hiring" is the specific combination that is *worst for someone who quits a job* — you're
unlikely to be laid off, but if you leave voluntarily, getting back in is hard. In a
high-churn market a failed venture costs you a few months. In this one it can cost a year.

With no runway, that single fact should shape every decision below.

## Ranked by how well each path survives a downturn

### 1. Union transit operator — strongest

Public sector, union contract, essential service. Recessions don't stop buses. Seniority
protects you against service reductions, and a defined-benefit pension is the one asset that
doesn't care what the market does.

**The real exposure isn't your job, it's the funding.** Washington transit leans heavily on
**sales tax revenue**, which is genuinely cyclical — a downturn hits agency budgets through
falling consumer spending. That shows up as service cuts, hiring freezes, and reduced
overtime long before it shows up as layoffs of senior operators.

**Practical read:** your job is safe; your *overtime* is not. Since overtime is the mechanism
funding every plan in this repo, a slowdown could stretch a 4-month savings timeline to 8.
Build that into the plan rather than being surprised by it.

### 2. Driving instruction and driver education — strong, and structurally unusual

**This is the standout, because the demand isn't economic — it's statutory.** You cannot get
a Washington license without the required education. That demand doesn't respond to GDP.

Three reinforcing factors:

- **HB 1878 expands the mandate on a legislated schedule** — 18-year-olds in 2027, phasing to
  age 21 by 2030. A recession doesn't repeal a statute.
- **The voucher program is counter-cyclical.** Public subsidy for eligible households means
  demand is supported *precisely when* families are squeezed.
- **Licenses are work infrastructure.** In a weak labor market, more people need a license to
  reach jobs, not fewer. Adult permit demand can rise in a downturn.

**The mild exposure:** families defer teen licensing when money is tight, so a downturn shifts
timing rather than eliminating demand. Instructor *employment* is stable but modestly paid —
it holds up, it just doesn't spike.

### 3. NEMT — excellent demand, but the policy risk is bigger than the recession risk

The demand side is close to recession-proof. **Dialysis patients travel three times a week
regardless of the economy** — it isn't discretionary in any sense. And Medicaid enrollment
*rises* in downturns as people lose employer coverage, which mechanically increases the
NEMT-eligible population.

**But the funding side moves the opposite direction, and that's the problem.**

**NEMT is an optional Medicaid benefit.** States are not required to cover it the way they
must cover hospital care. When state budgets tighten — which is exactly what a recession does
— optional benefits are the first place cuts land. You get rising demand and falling
reimbursement at the same time.

That's not hypothetical. Roughly **2 million Washingtonians rely on Apple Health, and an
estimated 250,000 are projected to lose coverage over the next ten years.** Fewer covered
people means fewer brokered trips, independent of any recession.

**The hedge is the thing King County already forced on you.** Because Hopelink won't contract
a startup, your year-one plan was private-pay and facility-direct anyway — dialysis centers,
assisted living, private families. **That book is insulated from Medicaid policy entirely.**
The constraint that looked like a setback is, on this axis, protection.

**Where a downturn does hurt you:** insurance is a fixed cost that bills whether the van moves
or not, and personally-guaranteed vehicle debt doesn't care about your trip volume. Fixed
costs are what kill undercapitalized operators in a slowdown — not demand.

### 4. CDL instruction and third-party examining — genuinely mixed

Two forces pull opposite ways:

- **Against:** freight is one of the most cyclical sectors there is. The 2022–2024 freight
  recession was severe. Weak freight means fewer new CDL drivers, fewer tests, less examiner
  volume.
- **For:** CDL school enrollment often *rises* in a downturn, because displaced workers retrain
  into trucking. Recessions push people toward exactly this credential.

**The stable version is the public-sector one.** Transit agencies and school districts need
operator and S-endorsement training regardless of the freight cycle — they're staffing buses,
not hauling freight. If you pursue instruction or examining, the agency and district side is
materially more durable than the private truck-school side.

### 5. Freight brokerage and owner-operator — most cyclical, avoid entering now

Spot rates are currently running 25%+ above last year with tender rejections above 15% — a
tight market. **That is the top of a cycle, not a floor.** Buying a truck into elevated rates
and elevated equipment prices is how owner-operators got destroyed in 2022–2024. I flagged
this earlier for capital reasons; the cycle argues the same way.

## What this changes about the plan

**It strengthens the case for what you were already going to do, and weakens the case for the
capital-intensive versions.**

**Do the things that are cheap, portable, and legally mandated:**

- The **free CDL instructor and third-party examiner registrations** cost nothing and can't be
  taken away by a downturn. A credential is the most recession-proof asset you can hold —
  it has no carrying cost.
- The **teen-driver instructor license** (~$318 + course) buys entry into the one sector here
  whose demand is written into statute and expanding on a schedule through 2030.
- **Endorsements** (hazmat, tanker) if you're pursuing driving work — weeks of effort, permanent.

**Defer the things with fixed costs and personal guarantees:**

- Commercial insurance at $5,500–$9,000 per vehicle per year bills in a bad month exactly as
  in a good one.
- A financed vehicle is a fixed payment against variable revenue.
- Hiring a W-2 driver adds payroll and L&I obligations that don't flex downward quickly.

**The sequencing already in `nemt/00-START-HERE.md` — keep the paycheck, validate for free,
spend only after demand is proven — is the correct posture for this macro environment, not
just for your finances.** A slowing economy with low layoffs and slow hiring is precisely the
wrong time to trade a secure W-2 for fixed business costs, and precisely the right time to
accumulate credentials while employed.

---

## The one number to watch

**Your overtime hours.** They're the funding mechanism for every plan here, and they're the
first thing to contract when transit budgets tighten. If your available overtime starts
falling, that's your earliest signal that the savings timeline needs to stretch — well before
anything shows up in national statistics.

Track it monthly. It's a better local indicator for your situation than any recession model.
